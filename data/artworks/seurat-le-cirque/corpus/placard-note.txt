The Cirque Fernando, Montmartre, by gaslight. A white horse canters the ring and a bareback rider balances on one slipper, arms open, skirt flaring like a struck match. An acrobat hangs at the top of his tumble. The ringmaster's whip draws one long arabesque across the sawdust. In the foreground a clown in red holds back the curtain with his back to us — we watch the show over his shoulder, from the cheap standing room. Behind the performers the audience rises in strict tiers graded by the price of the seats: top hats and gloves near the ring, caps and leaning elbows high in the gallery. Curved line against straight, spectacle against spectator. Orange against blue, dot against dot, tens of thousands of separate touches of pure colour mixed nowhere but in the eye. Lines that rise mean gaiety; warm colour means joy; the whole painting is a machine built by theory to manufacture delight. It was unfinished when Seurat hung it at the Indépendants in March 1891, and unfinished it stayed. He died that month at thirty-one. The dots never closed.
