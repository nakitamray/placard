September 1888, Arles, the bank of the Rhône, a few minutes' walk from the yellow house on the place Lamartine. The painter carries his easel to the river after dark and sets it down where the new gas lamps of the town burn across the water. Two kinds of light share the canvas: the stars of the Great Bear flaring green and rose in soft separated strokes, and the brutal gold of the gaslight dragged down through royal blue water in long molten columns. Between them the river turns like shot silk. At the water's edge, two lovers — the note of ordinary warmth he rarely left out. This is not the famous whirling night of Saint-Rémy, painted from memory in the asylum nine months later; this night was painted from the world, on the spot, with wet feet, by lamplight, by a painter who believed the night more alive and more richly coloured than the day, and who took care that you could find the constellation. Acquired by France in 1975, it hangs in the Musée d'Orsay, Paris, in the old station on the left bank, where the gold still burns across the blue.
