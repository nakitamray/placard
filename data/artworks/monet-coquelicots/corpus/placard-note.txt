A field above Argenteuil in high summer, a quarter of an hour by train from the Gare Saint-Lazare. The slope has gone red with wild poppies and the sky is all moving cloud. A woman with a parasol and a small boy stand at the crest against the sky; the same woman, the same boy, appear again at the foot of the slope, a minute later on the same walk, her parasol furled against her shoulder now, the child waist-deep in the grass with his arms full of flowers. Between the two pairs the eye takes the invisible diagonal path down the hillside without being asked. She is Camille, the painter's wife; the boy is Jean, their son. The poppies close to are only touches of vermilion, some absurdly large, floating free of any stem — the nearer the flower the bigger and looser the dab, until botany dissolves into a vibration of red against green. Painted in 1873, shown in April 1874 in Nadar's old studio on the boulevard des Capucines at the exhibition a mocking critic named impressionist, given to France with the Moreau-Nélaton bequest in 1906, and hanging now at the Musée d'Orsay, Paris, where the field is always in flower and the walk is never finished. Sunlight, wind in the grass, a red that refuses to stay still: the whole doctrine of painting out of doors, quickly, before the weather changes, folded into fifty centimetres of canvas.
