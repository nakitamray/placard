From a box above the stage of the Paris Opéra, looking down: the floor tips up behind her like a wall of amber light and the star floats against it, alone, taking her bow. Chin lifted, throat bare, eyes closed, one shoulder dipped, arms open — the révérence after a solo, held for the length of the applause. The footlights strike her from below, the gaslight that makes theatrical faces strange, and burn the front of her tutu to a white flare scattered with flower-colour. Behind the flats wait the others Degas always looked for: the shadowed legs of the corps de ballet under drooping skirts, and a man in black evening dress, faceless, hands in his pockets — an abonné, a subscriber, one of the men whose season tickets bought the run of the wings and whose favour could make a career. The fact is stated without comment, in the corner, where facts stood in the ballet's actual world. Pastel worked over a printed monotype gives the light its powdery flare. From the collection of Gustave Caillebotte, painter and friend, whose bequest of 1894 forced the state to hang the impressionists at last; now at the Musée d'Orsay, Paris, where the applause never ends and the bow is never finished.
